## Known limits

- A calendar reads the first 1,000 rows of the six weeks it shows; its days still count every row. A map shows the first 5,000 rows that have a place, and says so. A grouped grid shows its first 2,000 groups.
- Base snapshots, and a base copied through one, stop at 100,000 rows (row 83), and so do the direct download of a view, a field copied with its values, and an import that updates rows by key. A copied table keeps its rows' numbers and order, not their files. An import stops at the ceiling the operator sets (`IMPORT_MAX_ROWS`, 1,000,000 by default). An export job stops at the ceiling the operator sets (`EXPORT_MAX_ROWS`, 1,000,000 by default); it reads page after page by offset, so its last pages take longer. Export and snapshot files are kept for 24 hours.
- A failed or interrupted export or copy is retried by its author, from the tasks menu. An import is not: the rows it wrote before it stopped stay, and its author imports the file again.
- A field can require a value in AnyGridDB's own tables only (a client's database says itself what it requires), and not on a checkbox, a person, a file, or a link. Rows already empty when it becomes required stay so until that field is changed.
- A view saved while one of its fields is in the trash keeps that field's place and settings, but not its conditions inside a nested filter group, a colour rule, or a form field's condition.
- A base snapshot names people by email: a user cell keeps the people who are members of the new base. Roles given by groups stay behind.
- A collaborator with field or row rules finds in the row trash only the rows they deleted, named by their primary field when they may see it; on a client's table they have no row trash.
- Rows deleted before this release's many-to-many restore fix come back without those links.
- A SAML provider that sends only transient or address NameIDs matches accounts by address: it must verify them. OIDC, and SAML with a persistent NameID, bind the account to the provider's subject.
- The map view shows the rows of its view over a plain background, or over the tiles of the server an administrator names (ADR 0068); it does not pan or zoom.
- A synced table is as old as its last refresh, is read-only, stops at a million rows, and links to a table read live show empty.
- A locked client row is protected from AnyGridDB's writes only: a change another application makes is detected (within five minutes, seconds on a watched table) and told, not refused. Its seal covers every stored column, so a row put back still differs by its `updated_at`.
- On SQL Server a page is one query: a table with very many complex computed fields can exceed what SQL Server compiles, and AnyGridDB says so. There, MD5, SHA256, and SHA512 read texts of up to 100,000 characters, and JSON_EXTRACT reads a text value of up to 4,000.
- On Oracle an empty text is NULL, so a blank text and lists built from it read as nothing; `NUMBER` keeps no scale (`7.50` reads `7.5`); `DATE` reads as a day; regular expressions are refused, as on SQL Server. Only Oracle 23ai is tested; 19c is the floor (docs/sources.md).
- Regular expressions are a portable subset, written in the formula: `\d`, `\w`, and `\s` are ASCII, and back-references and lookbehinds are refused. SQL Server reads none before 2025, so there they are refused.
- JSON_EXTRACT needs PostgreSQL 16 or later, where a text that is not JSON can be told apart without failing the read. On MySQL it reads JSON numbers as doubles, and the field says so.
- The opt-in release check stays silent until an administrator turns it on and a GitHub Release exists; merges on `main` alone never raise the notice.

## Next

Phases 0 to 8 of the [migration plan](docs/migration-plan.md) are done;
the [decision records](docs/adr/README.md) say why each piece is as it
is. What is still missing comes in three tracks: the views and spreadsheet
gestures people miss, data in and out at any size, and what an instance
administrator cannot yet do from Administration. Inside a table, the order is the priority; across
tracks, the order of work below decides.

### Administration

An instance administrator should do everything from Administration, with
no command line and no access to the database: bring people in, choose
which bases each person reaches and with which role, see every base and
every outbound share, and keep the trails. Accounts, the security policy,
sources, and the trails are there; what is not comes in eight lots, in
this order, one pull request each (more when one would be too large to
read). Lot 8 is done on request only.

#### Lot 1: accounts

Done: people are added with roles on any base, have a page where their roles are given, changed, and taken away, and are deleted in one step (#67, #68, #12, #13, #18, #19, #20; ADR 0057, 0059). An administrator who makes someone an owner of a base tells the owners already there (#15).

#### Lot 2: the panel, by task

Done: six sections named by task, every page at an address of its own, a first page of what needs action, and entries in the sidebar and the command palette (#49, #26, #70).

#### Lot 3: bases and what leaves them

Every base, link, token, webhook, and automation of the instance, metadata only: never a cell value. Done: every public link and calendar feed in one list, under Sharing, revoked from there (#16), and every API token, webhook, and automation, revoked or switched off from there (#23). Every base in one list, with a page of its own: owners, members and their roles, tables, estimated rows, files, links and feeds (#14). From that page its members are given a role, changed, or removed without being one of them (#69), and the base is put in the trash; the bases no active account owns are pointed out, on the first page too, and the bases in the trash, whoever deleted them, are restored (#22). A base's last activity is not shown yet: the trail is indexed by table and time (migration 0068), so it is one probe per table of the base.

#### Lot 4: backups and alerts

Done: the backup script tells the instance how each run ended, and so can a backup made by other means; Health shows the last ones (#17). Administrators are told, on the first page of Administration, in Health, and by email, of a backup that failed or is late, storage nearly full, the instance's certificate close to expiry, a source whose watched tables cannot be read, and failures piling up: each with its threshold or turned off, once a day at most while it stays open, closed by itself once its cause is gone (#25). Each administrator chooses, in Health, the kinds they are told about by email, account locks among them; every alert still shows to all (#48). A source's own certificate is not watched: once it expires the source stops answering, and that is told.

#### Lot 5: groups and delegated roles

Each administration action declares the capability it needs. Done: before a change made in Administration, an administrator gives their password or second factor again, unless they did in the last five minutes. Every change asks, but the few that only take access away at once (ending a session, revoking a token or a link), try something without keeping it, or concern the administrator alone: one rule on the server, so a change added later asks without a word, and the page that makes it asks the person and sends it again; a wrong one counts toward the lock, and an account that signs in through its provider with a passkey alone signs in again (#28). Groups are made in Administration, with a name, a description, and members, beside those an identity provider sends, whose name and members stay its own; any group is given a role on a base from there (#27). An account's page says what the account may do on each base and each table, and whether the role is its own or a group's (#29). An auditor, a role an administrator gives with the same proof, reads every page of Administration, exports its trails and who has access, and changes nothing: the pages show their controls switched off, and the server refuses whatever is not a read (#34, ADR 0071). An administrator who must read a base they are not a member of opens it for an hour, with a reason: they read every table and change nothing, it is written to the change trail, and the base's owners are told at once, in the app and by email, who reads their base, why, and until when. An administrator who gives themselves a role on a base gives a reason too, and the owners are told the same way (#35, ADR 0074).

## Non-goals

- SDK, secrets CLI, and Helm in this tree
- AnyGridDB's own store on anything but PostgreSQL (ADR 0003). MySQL, MariaDB, SQL Server, and Oracle come in as external sources (ADRs 0006, 0024)
- A paid or enterprise build
- MinIO (abandoned upstream), or any non-OSI default
